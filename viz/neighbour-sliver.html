Why a Lorentz-invariant point has infinitely many neighbours

Dots are spacetime points scattered at random with uniform density. The coloured dots are the origin's "one-tick neighbours": every point in its future that is at most ε of proper time away. Zoom out and they keep appearing along the light cone. Boost and they slide along the hyperbola, but none go missing.

Step 1

The hyperbola is Minkowski's circle

In ordinary space, "within distance ε" means inside a circle. In spacetime, "within ε ticks of proper time" means between the light cone and the hyperbola t² − x² = ε². The hyperbola never closes. It runs off to infinity, hugging the cone.

Step 2

The sliver thins like 1/x, but too slowly

At distance x, the sliver's height is √(x² + ε²) − x ≈ ε²/(2x). Adding up its area is like the harmonic series 1 + ½ + ⅓ + … which never stops growing.

area(X) ≈ ε²/2 + ε²·ln(2X/ε) → ∞
Step 3

Every band is a boosted copy of the others

Each coloured band spans one unit of rapidity and has the same area, ε²/2. A boost maps one band exactly onto the next. Move the boost slider and watch the bands trade places. Rapidity has no end, so there are infinitely many bands and infinitely many neighbours.

Step 4

Why a crystal escapes, and pays for it

A crystal links each point to a few neighbours only by choosing which band counts as "nearest". That choice is a preferred frame. Keep all frames equal and you must keep all bands, so every point touches infinitely many others: non-locality.
